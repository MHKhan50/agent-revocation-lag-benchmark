# RevocationLag: Measuring stale-authority exposure in multi-step AI-agent workflows

*Reproducible synthetic benchmark — 27 October 2026*

## Abstract
AI agents increasingly act through tools, making the timing of authorization checks an important deployment property. A permission that was valid at workflow start may be revoked while a plan is still running. This note introduces RevocationLag, a small synthetic benchmark comparing four enforcement strategies: one-time authorization, periodic re-checking, checks before sensitive actions, and an idealized immediate revocation notification. The experiment measures unauthorized sensitive actions, blocking of revoked actions, completion of still-authorized work, and control-event overhead. It is a reproducible baseline for students and researchers, not a claim about observed behaviour of deployed agents.

## Motivation and research question
The University of Surrey announced an ARIA-backed project on 15 October 2026 to develop a secure sandbox for AI agents, including permissions that can change or be removed while agents operate. The Alan Turing Institute's CETaS published a behavioural-assurance paper on 23 September 2026 emphasising continuous monitoring, deployment context, and reassessment of permissions. The University of Glasgow's March 2026 partnership with Lloyds Banking Group plans empirical evaluation of agentic AI in software and data engineering. These are evidence of current research activity, not proof that every aspect of dynamic authorization is unexplored.

**Research question:** How does authorization-check granularity affect unauthorized side effects after a tool permission is revoked during a multi-step workflow, and what control-event overhead accompanies each strategy?

## Method
The included generator creates 1,800 deterministic synthetic action traces across seeds 17, 29, and 43. Each trace contains benign actions (`B`), sensitive actions (`S`) requiring a capability, a fixed plan length, and a revocation point. Revocation takes effect before the action at that index. All four policies run on the same traces.

The one-time policy checks at the beginning and then relies on cached permission. The periodic policy re-checks every three action positions. The per-sensitive policy checks before every sensitive action. The event-driven baseline receives an idealized immediate notification when revocation occurs. This baseline is intentionally optimistic: delivery failures and delays are outside the present simulator.

Metrics are computed from action traces, not from an LLM. “Control events” combines authorization checks and revocation notifications; it is a proxy for control overhead, not a measurement of wall-clock latency, cost, or user experience. “Authorized-work completion” counts only actions that remain permitted after revocation, so correctly blocked revoked actions are not counted as failure to complete authorized work.

These figures describe the stated synthetic simulator only. They are not estimates of real deployment risk. Policies that retain stale permission can execute revoked sensitive actions; policies that re-check or receive a revocation event should block them once they learn about revocation. Control-event counts do not establish production latency or operational cost.

## Limitations and next steps
This is a policy-level experiment. It does not execute a real agent, call an external tool, model prompt injection, simulate network failures, or prove a sandbox secure. The immediate-notification baseline assumes perfect delivery. The single capability and fixed plan omit concurrent actions, queued requests, retries, distributed caches, and partial failure.

A stronger follow-up could add delayed or lost revocation events, queued tool calls, multiple capability classes, different checkpoint intervals, and action-severity weights. If a real agent framework is added, use mock tools or an isolated local sandbox, log every action, and compare traces against a pre-registered authorization oracle. A literature review is required before claiming novelty.

## Reproducibility
Run `python3 run_benchmark.py` and `python3 -m unittest discover -s tests -v`. The package uses Python's standard library and fixed seeds. Code is MIT-licensed and the generated CSV is dedicated to CC0 1.0.
